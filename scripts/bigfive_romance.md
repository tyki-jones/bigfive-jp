# Big Five Personality Traits and Romantic Relationships: Curated Reference for Narrative Content

*Curated from the original research reference document. Sections were kept, trimmed, or removed based on the criteria below, with **long-term user satisfaction** as the primary optimization target — since this is a personality app whose narratives users will read about themselves and often share.*

## Curation Criteria

A finding was kept only if it passed all of these:

1. **Evidentiary strength** — meta-analytic or multiply-replicated. Single-study, small-sample (n<100), or explicitly "contested/inconsistent" findings were cut unless the risk of misuse was near-zero and the caveat could stay attached.
2. **Actionability** — translates into constructive guidance a user can actually do something with, not just a verdict about their fate.
3. **Non-alarming / non-deterministic** — nothing implying a user is statistically headed for divorce, infidelity, or romantic failure because of a trait score. A personality-test narrative should never read like a diagnosis of relationship doom.
4. **Non-stigmatizing** — no content a user could feel shamed by, screenshot and use against a partner, or that singles out singles/low scorers as deficient. No gender-essentialist claims.
5. **Cultural relevance** — Japan-specific findings were prioritized and kept even when the sample sizes were modest, because the alternative (silence) means defaulting to WEIRD assumptions for a Japanese audience.
6. **Hook value** — genuinely surprising findings were kept if well-evidenced, because they make narratives feel insightful rather than generic.

**Cut entirely:** infidelity/cheating predictors (stigmatizing, contested, high potential for user harm), divorce/breakup risk statistics (deterministic, alarming), gender-difference findings (small, inconsistent, risk of essentialism), singlehood/dating-initiation findings (risk of shaming single users), and single-study "surprising" claims that added confusion without enough evidentiary weight to justify the risk.

## TL;DR

- The most robust finding across meta-analyses and large longitudinal studies: **your own personality — especially low Neuroticism, high Agreeableness, and high Conscientiousness — predicts relationship satisfaction far more than your partner's personality or how similar you two are.** Actor effects account for ~6% of variance in satisfaction, partner effects 1–3%, and couple similarity "consistently explains less than 0.5% of variance" (Dyrenforth, Kashy, Donnellan & Lucas, 2010, *JPSP*).
- **"Opposites attract" is essentially a myth for personality; "like attracts like" is weakly true at partner selection but doesn't improve satisfaction.**
- For a Japanese audience: the literature is overwhelmingly Western. The clearest Japan-specific study (Abe & Oshio, 2018) suggests **partner effects may matter more in Japan's interdependent culture, and personality similarity is not required for — and may even reduce — satisfaction.**

## Key Findings

1. **Neuroticism is the strongest and most consistent personality predictor of relationship dissatisfaction** — in oneself and, to a smaller degree, one's partner. Well-replicated across meta-analyses (Heller, Watson & Ilies, 2004; Malouff et al., 2010; Karney & Bradbury, 1995).
2. **Agreeableness and Conscientiousness are the "universally good" partner traits**, followed by low Neuroticism. These show the strongest *partner effects* — they benefit whoever you're with, regardless of that person's own traits.
3. **Actor effects >> partner effects >> similarity effects.** Your own traits predict ~6% of satisfaction variance; partner traits 1–3%; similarity <0.5% (Dyrenforth et al., 2010).
4. **Extraversion is mildly positive for satisfaction** and for how easily relationships form.
5. **Openness plays a minor, inconsistent role** in satisfaction, but is the trait couples tend to be most similar on.
6. **Relationship-specific perceptions (felt commitment, appreciation, intimacy) predict satisfaction far better than personality does** — Joel et al. (2020, *PNAS*) found these dwarf trait-based predictors.

## 1. Trait Similarity vs. Complementarity ("Do opposites attract?")

**The verdict: similarity weakly holds at partner selection; "opposites attract" is essentially a myth for personality; and actual similarity barely moves satisfaction.**

- **Assortative mating is real but weak for personality.** Horwitz, Balbona, Paulich & Keller (2023, *Nature Human Behaviour*), ~23,000 spousal pairs: significant positive partner correlations for every Big Five trait, but personality had the *weakest* assortment of all traits studied — couples assort far more strongly on age, political/religious attitudes, and education than on personality (Openness r=.21, Conscientiousness .16, Agreeableness .11, Neuroticism .11, Extraversion .08).
- **There is essentially no evidence for "opposites attract" in personality.** Reviews consistently find "consistent evidence for similarity but very little evidence for opposites" (Watson et al., 2004; Luo & Klohnen, 2005).
- **Actual similarity barely predicts satisfaction.** Dyrenforth et al. (2010), three nationally representative samples (Australia N=5,278; UK N=6,554; Germany N=11,418): couple similarity "consistently explains less than 0.5% of the variance in relationship satisfaction" after controlling for actor and partner effects.
- **Perceived similarity matters more than actual similarity**, and couples tend to perceive themselves as more similar than they really are (Luo & Snider, 2009) — actual similarity mostly matters "at zero acquaintance" (early dating), not in established relationships (Montoya, Horton & Kirchner, 2008).

*This is a strong, low-risk, high-value hook: it lets narratives reassure users that a "mismatched" score with their partner is not a red flag, and that trait-matching apps/quizzes overstate the importance of similarity.*

## 2. Which Traits Predict Satisfaction, Commitment, and Longevity

- **Malouff, Thorsteinsson, Schutte, Bhullar & Rooke (2010, *JRP*)**, 19 samples, N=3,848: a partner's low Neuroticism, high Agreeableness, high Conscientiousness, and high Extraversion each predicted one's own satisfaction. Approximate partner-effect sizes: Neuroticism −.22, Agreeableness .15, Conscientiousness .12, Extraversion .06, Openness .03.
- **Karney & Bradbury (1995, *Psychological Bulletin*)**, meta-analysis of longitudinal studies: each partner's Neuroticism accounted for roughly 10% of variability in later marital satisfaction.
- **Actor effects reliably exceed partner effects.** Dyrenforth et al. (2010): actor effects ~6% of variance; partner effects (largest for Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, emotional stability) 1–3%.
- **Conscientiousness gains importance over time, and personality matures with relationships.** Claxton, O'Rourke, Smith & DeLongis (2012), 125 long-wed couples (avg. 34 years): Conscientiousness — not Neuroticism — was the trait most broadly associated with satisfaction in long marriages. Separately, well-replicated lifespan research shows Conscientiousness tends to rise and Neuroticism tends to fall as relationships mature — i.e., the traits that most affect satisfaction are also the ones most likely to improve with time.
- **Why neuroticism hurts:** mediated partly by sexual dissatisfaction (Russell & McNulty, 2008) and by negative-interpretation biases in day-to-day communication — meaning the mechanism is about *patterns*, not a fixed trait doomed to cause problems.

## 3. Trait-Specific Compatibility Notes

- **Two highly anxious/moody partners can amplify each other's stress cycles** — this is a fairly intuitive and low-risk pattern worth naming gently (frame as "two high-Neuroticism partners may need to be more intentional about calming conflict," not as a warning).
- **Both partners' traits contribute independently, not interactively** (Robins, Caspi & Moffitt, 2000, 360 couples) — each partner's personality adds its own effect rather than multiplying risk. This is a useful, reassuring framing: your partner's traits and your own aren't multiplying each other's flaws.
- *Removed:* narrow single-study findings on neuroticism-mismatch-by-gender, extraversion-mismatch-post-children, and openness-mismatch — evidence was inconsistent or too thin to state confidently in a personalized narrative.

## 4. Behavioral Patterns by Trait in Relationships

- **Higher Neuroticism in a partner is linked to more conflict-avoidance, withholding, and difficulty resolving disagreements constructively** (Heaven et al., 2006). Useful for constructive advice ("naming the specific worry out loud, rather than withdrawing, tends to help").
- **The demand-withdraw conflict pattern** (one partner pushes for discussion, the other disengages) is robustly linked to relationship distress (Christensen & Heavey, 1990) — but importantly, **it is not a fixed personality trait.** It's shaped by who holds more power in the relationship and whose issue is being discussed. This is a genuinely useful, non-blaming reframe: the pattern is situational, not a life sentence tied to someone's personality.
- **Jealousy:** higher Neuroticism, lower Agreeableness, and lower Openness predicted higher romantic jealousy in a reasonably sized study (Richter et al., 2022, N=847); attachment anxiety partially explains neuroticism's link to jealousy. Framing should be constructive (e.g., naming what tends to trigger jealousy and how to communicate it) rather than "your traits make you jealous."

*Removed: the infidelity/cheating-prediction literature in its entirety. Even though some individual findings (e.g., low Conscientiousness as a predictor) have moderate evidentiary support, this content is high-risk for a personalized narrative product — it's stigmatizing, easy to misread as an accusation, evidentially inconsistent across studies, and has no constructive framing that doesn't feel like a warning label on a person. The downside (a user reading "people like you are more likely to cheat") far outweighs any narrative value.*

## 5. What Traits to Look for in a Long-Term Partner

**The research is unusually clear here: independent of your own personality, an emotionally stable (low-Neuroticism), Agreeable, and Conscientious partner is a genuinely good bet.** These traits produce the largest, most consistent partner effects.

- **Partner Conscientiousness even predicts your career outcomes.** Solomon & Jackson (2014, *Psychological Science*, N=4,544): a more conscientious partner predicted one's own future job satisfaction, income, and promotions — independent of gender and one's own conscientiousness.
- **The big caveat, which should always accompany this section:** Joel et al. (2020, *PNAS*, 43 datasets, 11,196 couples) found a partner's self-reported traits explained only about 5% of variance in the other partner's satisfaction. Relationship-specific factors (felt commitment, appreciation, intimacy) predicted far more — up to 45% at baseline. **Traits are a mild tilt in your favor, not a formula.**

## 6. Cultural Context (Japan / East Asia)

**The broader literature is overwhelmingly Western, so this section should be weighted heavily when localizing narratives.**

- **Abe & Oshio (2018, *SAGE Open*), N=749 married Japanese couples** — the most directly relevant study available: actor effects mirror Western findings (higher Agreeableness, Extraversion, Conscientiousness and lower Neuroticism → higher own satisfaction). But **partner effects were especially prominent**, consistent with Japan's more interdependent relational culture. Strikingly, **dissimilarity** in Agreeableness, Neuroticism, and Openness predicted *higher* satisfaction, and in shorter marriages similar couples were somewhat *less* satisfied.
- **Similarity is perceived as lower — and matters less — in East Asia generally.** Cross-cultural World Values Survey analysis found East Asians (China + Japan) viewed themselves as less similar to their spouses than Westerners did, yet this did not translate into the similarity-satisfaction link Western researchers often assume.

*Use this section to justify a narrative stance that is genuinely well-suited to a Japanese audience: users should not worry about matching their partner's trait profile, and personalized advice should lean on "what makes you a good partner" and "what to appreciate in your partner" rather than compatibility scoring.*

## Recommendations for Narrative Generation

1. **Anchor every romance narrative on the actor-effect principle:** a user's own emotional stability, agreeableness, and conscientiousness are the biggest levers on their *own* romantic happiness. This is empowering, well-evidenced, and puts agency in the user's hands.
2. **For high-Neuroticism users, frame constructively.** Neuroticism is the strongest satisfaction-related risk factor, but it's also one of the more changeable traits — it tends to decline with age and relationship maturity. Emphasize naming worries directly and communication skills. Never use language implying doom.
3. **For partner-selection content:** recommend valuing agreeableness, conscientiousness, and emotional stability in a partner, but always pair this with the humility caveat — a partner's traits explain only a small slice of satisfaction; relationship-specific closeness matters far more.
4. **Debunk "opposites attract" and "you must be similar."** Both are weak or false for personality — this is a strong, low-risk, high-interest hook, and especially fitting for a Japanese audience given the local findings.
5. **Never generate content implying a user is more likely to cheat, divorce, or fail at relationships because of their trait scores.** These predictions exist in the literature but are inconsistent, easy to misapply to an individual, and actively harmful to include in a product people will read about themselves.
6. **Avoid gendered claims** ("men with trait X..." / "women who score high in Y..."). The evidence for gender differences in this literature is weak and inconsistent, and the app scores individuals, not genders.
7. **Use the demand-withdraw reframe as a model for tone:** describe conflict patterns as situational and workable, not as fixed consequences of someone's personality.

## Caveats

- **Effect sizes are small.** Personality explains single-digit percentages of satisfaction variance — narratives should never imply certainty.
- **WEIRD bias is severe** — the field is overwhelmingly Western. Japan-specific Big Five relationship research is essentially one solid study (Abe & Oshio, 2018) plus supporting cross-cultural work; claims should be hedged accordingly.
- **Causality is unclear** in most of this literature — personality both shapes and is shaped by relationships. Avoid narrative language that implies a fixed, one-directional trait → outcome relationship.
