# Big Five Personality Traits and Career/Occupational Outcomes: Curated Reference for Narrative Content

*Curated from the original research reference document. Sections were kept, trimmed, or removed based on the criteria below, with **long-term user satisfaction** as the primary optimization target — since this is a personality app whose narratives users will read about themselves and often share.*

## Curation Criteria

A finding was kept only if it passed all of these:

1. **Evidentiary strength** — meta-analytic or multiply-replicated. Single-study, small-sample, or explicitly "could not verify" findings were cut, unless flagged clearly and kept only for genuine cultural-relevance or hook reasons.
2. **Actionability** — translates into constructive guidance a user can actually do something with, not just a verdict about their earning potential or career ceiling.
3. **Non-alarming / non-deterministic** — nothing implying a user is statistically locked out of leadership, entrepreneurship, or high income because of a trait score. A personality-test narrative should never read like a career prognosis.
4. **Non-stigmatizing** — no content that pits traits against each other as "winners and losers" in dollar terms, no gendered income/promotion claims, nothing a user could feel judged or boxed in by.
5. **Cultural relevance** — Japan-specific findings were prioritized and kept even when sample sizes were modest, because this is a Japanese-facing app and several Western findings actually *reverse* in Japan — silence here would mean defaulting to WEIRD assumptions that are actively wrong for this audience.
6. **Hook value** — genuinely surprising findings were kept if well-evidenced (or clearly flagged as single-study), because they make narratives feel insightful rather than generic.

**Cut or trimmed:** the specific gendered dollar-figure statistics behind the Western "agreeableness income penalty" (deterministic, stigmatizing, and directly contradicted by the Japan-specific data this app actually needs), the "nice guys finish last... victory that may come at a cost" framing (moralizing/stigmatizing), and the remote-work personality claims (explicitly flagged in the source as unverified/thin evidence). Everything else was substantially well-evidenced and low-risk, so most of the document survives — this doc runs safer than the romance reference to begin with.

## TL;DR
- **Conscientiousness is the one near-universal predictor of job performance across essentially all occupations (Barrick & Mount 1991; corrected validity ~.20–.22), but every other trait has strong job-specific value** — Extraversion for sales/management/leadership emergence, Openness for creative and adaptive roles, and Emotional Stability (low Neuroticism) for high-stress and high-performance contexts.
- **"More" is not always "better."** Extreme Conscientiousness, Extraversion, and assertiveness show inverted-U (diminishing/reversing) effects, and "follow your passion" is contradicted by research favoring a "develop your passion" growth mindset — both are reassuring, non-deterministic framings.
- **Japan/East Asia research is genuinely sparse but shows real reversals from WEIRD findings**: Agreeableness predicts *higher* income for Japanese men (Lee & Ohtake 2018) — opposite of the Western penalty — and Extraversion/Agreeableness are stronger leadership predictors in collectivist cultures (Javalagi, Newman & Li 2024). This should be weighted heavily in any Japan-facing narrative.

## Key Findings

1. **Conscientiousness** is the only Big Five trait that predicts performance across all job types; it is the backbone of "success" narratives but should be framed as an effort/reliability foundation, not a personality virtue.
2. **Extraversion** predicts leadership *emergence* far more than leadership *effectiveness*, and predicts *choosing* sales more than *succeeding* at sales — a key gap between visibility and results, and a genuinely reassuring one for quieter users.
3. **Openness** is the single best predictor of creativity and innovation, but also predicts job-hopping and lower stability — a real trade-off, not a flaw.
4. **Agreeableness** is largely irrelevant to performance in most jobs, and its relationship to income and leadership emergence is culturally reversed between the West and Japan — it helps once someone is *in* a leadership role, and appears clearly beneficial in Japanese/East Asian collectivist contexts specifically.
5. **Neuroticism / Emotional Stability** is the second-most consistent performance predictor and the strongest personality driver of burnout and low job satisfaction — but it's also one of the more changeable traits over time.
6. Nonlinear ("too-much-of-a-good-thing") effects are real for Conscientiousness, Extraversion, and Emotional Stability — this supports framing every trait level, including "very high," around an *optimal* range rather than "more is always better."
7. Person-environment fit predicts satisfaction and retention more than raw trait levels — the right environment matters as much as the trait itself.
8. Personality matures with age (rising Conscientiousness/Agreeableness, falling Neuroticism), reshaping fit over a career — nothing about a 20-year-old's profile is fixed for life.

## 1. Trait–Occupation Fit and the Big Five × RIASEC Crossover

The foundational meta-analysis crossing personality with vocational interests is **Barrick, Mount & Gupta (2003, *Personnel Psychology*)**, building on **Larson, Rottinghaus & Borgen (2002)** and **De Fruyt & Mervielde (1997)**. These converge on five substantial Big Five–RIASEC correlations:

- **Artistic–Openness (r ≈ .48)** — the strongest link; creative/artistic fields select strongly on Openness.
- **Enterprising–Extraversion (r ≈ .41)** — sales, management, entrepreneurship, persuasion roles.
- **Social–Extraversion (r ≈ .31)** — teaching, counseling, helping professions.
- **Investigative–Openness (r ≈ .28)** — scientific research, analysis.
- **Social–Agreeableness (r ≈ .19)** — caregiving/helping.

The **Realistic (skilled trades, mechanical) and Conventional (clerical, finance/administrative, rule-following) types show no substantial correlation with any Big Five trait** — a well-replicated null across three separate meta-analyses. This is a genuinely useful, non-alarming point: personality-based narratives for trades and administrative fields should lean on Conscientiousness (performance) rather than implying a "fit" or "mismatch" that isn't supported by the data.

**Established occupational profiles** (Zhao & Seibert 2006; UK Household Longitudinal Study, N≈20,000): entrepreneurs and managers both score high Openness, high Conscientiousness, high Extraversion, and low Neuroticism; entrepreneurs distinctively score *lower Agreeableness* than managers, and supervisors are mainly characterized by high Conscientiousness.

## 2. Job Performance Meta-Analyses

**Barrick & Mount (1991, *Personnel Psychology*, 44:1–26)** — the landmark study, 117 studies, five occupational groups. **Conscientiousness predicted performance across all groups and all criteria. Extraversion predicted performance only in socially interactive occupations (managers, sales). Openness and Extraversion both predicted training proficiency.** Sample-size-weighted mean validity for Conscientiousness: r̄ = .22.

**Replications:** Salgado (1997) in European samples (Conscientiousness ~.20, Emotional Stability ~.13); Hurtz & Donovan (2000) confirmed both at generalizable levels (90% credibility intervals excluding zero); Barrick, Mount & Judge (2001) and a 2019 second-order meta-analysis reaffirmed Conscientiousness as the strongest, most universal predictor.

**Why this holds up so well:** it's consistent across cultures, job levels, and measurement methods, because job performance universally requires sustained voluntary effort. **Emotional Stability (low Neuroticism) is the second-most consistent cross-occupational predictor.**

**Job-specific validity, useful for tailoring by role:**
- **Extraversion** → sales, management, socially interactive roles.
- **Openness** → training performance, creative/adaptive/learning-intensive roles.
- **Emotional Stability** → high-stress jobs, roles requiring consistent performance under pressure.
- **Agreeableness** → teamwork-intensive and caregiving roles.

**Job complexity as a moderator:** personality generally matters more in complex, autonomous jobs where people have discretion over how they work, and less in highly structured "strong situation" jobs.

## 3. Income, Promotion, and Leadership Attainment

**Earnings meta-analysis (Alderotti, Rapallini & Traverso 2023; 62 articles, 896 effect sizes):** Openness, Conscientiousness, and Extraversion are positively associated with earnings; Agreeableness and Neuroticism negatively — in **Western samples**. Effects are small-to-modest and shrink substantially once IQ, education, and family background are controlled for. **This Western pattern should not be presented as the headline finding for a Japanese audience — see Section 10, where it reverses for Japanese men.**

**Career-success meta-analysis (Ng, Eby, Sorensen & Feldman 2005):** personality predicts *subjective* career success (how satisfied you feel) more strongly than *objective* success (salary, promotions), which is driven more by human capital and circumstance. Neuroticism is a modest barrier to salary and promotions; Openness a modest boost to income.

**Leadership (Judge, Bono, Ilies & Gerhardt 2002):** meta-analysis of 222 correlations, 73 samples. Corrected correlations with leadership: **Extraversion .31, Conscientiousness .28, Neuroticism −.24, Openness .24, Agreeableness .08.** Extraversion was the most consistent correlate, especially of *emergence* (who gets seen as a leader) rather than *effectiveness* (how well they actually lead once there) — a genuinely useful distinction that avoids overselling extraversion as a leadership requirement. Agreeableness's link to leadership is weak in Western samples specifically — but per Section 10, this is one of the traits that flips in collectivist/Japanese contexts.

**Nonlinear ("too much of a good thing") effects:** Le et al. (2011) and Carter et al. (2014) found curvilinear relationships between Conscientiousness/Emotional Stability and performance — beyond a threshold, more stopped helping and could even hurt. Ames & Flynn (2007) found the same for assertiveness and leadership (too little *and* too much both hurt). **This directly supports writing "very high" trait narratives around optimal ranges and gentle trade-offs, not unconditional praise.**

## 4. Entrepreneurship and Risk-Tolerant Careers

**Zhao & Seibert (2006)** and **Zhao, Seibert & Lumpkin (2010)** meta-analyses: entrepreneurs score higher on Openness and Conscientiousness, and lower on Neuroticism and Agreeableness, than managers or the general population. High Openness and Conscientiousness were the strongest predictors of both entrepreneurial intent and performance.

**Interpretation, framed as a tendency rather than a requirement:** the entrepreneurial profile tends toward high Openness (spotting opportunity), high Conscientiousness (follow-through, linked to venture survival), high Extraversion (selling the vision), low Neuroticism (tolerating uncertainty), and lower Agreeableness (protecting limited resources, driving hard bargains). This is a real pattern, not a gate — plenty of successful entrepreneurs don't fit it, and it's a poor fit for a personalized narrative to read as "you can't start a business."

## 5. Niche and Atypical Personality–Job Matches (flagged as single-study but genuinely useful, low-risk hooks)

- **The ambivert advantage in sales (Grant 2013, *Psychological Science*, N=340):** extraversion had essentially zero correlation with sales performance; the relationship was curvilinear, and **ambiverts outsold both introverts (~24% more) and extraverts (~32% more).** A widely-cited single study, but genuinely reassuring — it directly contradicts the "born salesperson = extravert" stereotype.
- **Introverted leaders can outperform (Grant, Gino & Hofmann 2011):** extraverted leaders got better results with passive teams, but **introverted leaders got better results with proactive employees**, because they listen and let initiative flourish rather than dominating. A good counterweight to any narrative that implies quieter users are less suited to leadership.
- **Neuroticism has situational functional value:** Feist (1998) found relatively *high* Neuroticism common among creative artists, plausibly tied to emotional sensitivity feeding the work. Worth a brief, hedged mention for high-Neuroticism users — never as a substitute for acknowledging Neuroticism's net-negative relationship with most outcomes, just as one honest counterpoint.

## 6. Career Satisfaction vs. Performance Divergence

**Job satisfaction meta-analysis (Judge, Heller & Mount 2002):** true-score correlations with satisfaction: **Neuroticism −.29, Extraversion .25, Conscientiousness .26, Agreeableness .17, Openness .02.**

**The divergence, framed constructively:** Conscientiousness predicts both performance and satisfaction. **Openness predicts creativity/performance in innovative fields yet is nearly unrelated to job satisfaction** — high-Openness people can excel while still feeling restless, which is a useful thing for a narrative to name rather than gloss over. **Neuroticism barely affects performance but meaningfully lowers satisfaction** — worth stating as "your wellbeing at work may need more active attention than your output does," not as "you'll always be unhappy at work."

**Person-environment fit theory:** Holland's congruence theory, and Schneider's Attraction-Selection-Attrition framework, both support that long-run satisfaction depends on fit between a person's traits and their specific environment — a "poor fit" by conventional standards can still be genuinely satisfying in the right environment. Kristof-Brown, Zimmerman & Johnson's (2005) meta-analysis confirms person-job and person-organization fit strongly predict satisfaction and retention. **This is one of the most useful non-deterministic framings available: fit, not trait level alone, drives outcomes.**

## 7. Trait-Specific Workplace Behavior

- **Conscientiousness:** reliability, planning, persistence, low absenteeism/turnover; leadership via structure. At the extreme: perfectionism, rigidity, over-deliberation.
- **Extraversion:** assertiveness, networking, employee voice, leadership emergence; thrives in collaborative/team environments.
- **Openness:** best predictor of creativity/innovation and adaptability, but linked to higher turnover and job-hopping — the flip side of the same trait.
- **Agreeableness:** teamwork, cooperation, buffers against burnout; weaker negotiation outcomes (tends to concede more).
- **Neuroticism:** strongest personality driver of burnout (Alarcon, Eschleman & Bowling 2009, N>45,000; Swider & Zimmerman 2010, ρ=.52 for emotional exhaustion) and turnover intention.
- **Turnover (Zimmerman 2008 meta-analytic path model):** Emotional Stability best predicts (negatively) intentions to quit; Conscientiousness and Agreeableness best predict (negatively) actual turnover; high Openness predicts unplanned quitting.

## 8. Career Change, Maturation, and Life-Stage Effects

**The maturity principle (Roberts, Walton & Viechtbauer 2006; 92 longitudinal studies):** with age, especially 20–40, people rise in Conscientiousness and decline in Neuroticism; Agreeableness rises later (after ~50). People with more successful/satisfying early careers increase disproportionately in Emotional Stability and Conscientiousness — success and personality maturation reinforce each other.

**This is one of the most valuable, reassuring findings in the whole document for narrative use:** a restless, low-Conscientiousness user in their 20s will typically become measurably more conscientious and emotionally stable over time. High Openness predicts a higher likelihood of career changes and upward pivots — framed as adaptability, not instability.

## 9. Surprising / Counterintuitive Findings (genuine hooks, kept because well-evidenced or clearly flagged)

- **"Follow your passion" is contradicted by research.** O'Keefe, Dweck & Walton (2018, *Psychological Science*): a *fixed* "find your passion" mindset leads to narrower interests and abandonment when work gets hard; a *growth* "develop your passion" mindset sustains motivation. Strongly recommended framing for all career narratives, regardless of trait profile.
- **Extraversion is not required for effective leadership** — it predicts who gets noticed, not who leads well.
- **Openness correlates with career mobility, not instability for its own sake** — the flip side of creativity.
- **More is not always better** — inverted-U effects for Conscientiousness, Extraversion, and assertiveness.
- **The best salespeople are ambiverts, not extraverts.**
- **A partner's — or, per Solomon & Jackson (2014, N=4,544) — a spouse's Conscientiousness predicts *your* future income, satisfaction, and promotions**, independent of your own traits. A genuinely fun, well-evidenced crossover hook if the app ever wants to connect the career and romance sections.

## 10. Cultural Context: Japan / East Asia (the most important section for this app — weight heavily)

Most personality-vocational research uses Western samples, and Japan-specific evidence is comparatively sparse — but the studies that exist show real, meaningful reversals that make the Western findings above actively misleading if applied unmodified to a Japanese audience.

- **Agreeableness income reversal (Lee & Ohtake 2018, N from Japanese survey data):** Agreeableness is associated with **higher** income for Japanese male workers — the opposite of the Western penalty — strongest at large Japanese firms, and operating through productivity rather than promotion specifically. For promotion to management, Extraversion mattered more than any other trait in both countries.
- **Management attainment (Lee & Ohtake 2012, N=4,852 Japanese, N=3,653 US):** Extraversion positively predicted holding a management position for Japanese men, with no equivalent effect in the US sample studied.
- **Leadership in collectivist cultures (Javalagi, Newman & Li 2024; meta-analysis, emergence k=120/N=32,579, effectiveness k=116/N=42,487):** both Extraversion and **Agreeableness** were stronger predictors of leadership effectiveness in collectivist (including East Asian) cultures than in individualist ones.
- **Collectivist job satisfaction (Templer 2012, N=354, Singapore):** Agreeableness had a clear positive relationship with job satisfaction, unlike the near-null Western finding — supporting that harmony-relevant traits matter more in East Asian workplaces generally.
- **Work engagement in Japan (Kawai et al. 2024, N=1,500 Japanese workers):** high Conscientiousness boosted engagement when job control was high; low Neuroticism *and* low Extraversion were both linked to higher engagement — the low-Extraversion result runs counter to typical Western findings and is worth noting as a genuine cultural difference rather than smoothing it over.
- **Managerial performance (Robie et al., US/Japan comparison — single study, flagged accordingly):** Extraversion was the strongest correlate of predicted performance and Conscientiousness the weakest among Japanese managers studied — a reversal of the usual Western ordering.

**Bottom line for Japan-facing narratives:** do not assume Western findings transfer by default. Agreeableness should be framed as a career asset, not a liability, for a Japanese audience. Extraversion may matter more for both leadership and performance than the Western literature suggests. State plainly, where relevant, that the Japan-specific evidence base is still thin — but the direction of these reversals is consistent enough across multiple independent studies to weight this section heavily rather than defaulting to the Western baseline.

## Recommendations

1. **Anchor every trait's "career strengths" section on the meta-analytically robust findings**: Conscientiousness→performance everywhere; Extraversion→sales/leadership emergence/social roles; Openness→creativity/innovation/adaptation; Emotional Stability→stress tolerance/satisfaction; Agreeableness→teamwork/caregiving (and, for this audience, likely income and leadership too — see below).
2. **Use the 5-level structure to encode nonlinearity, not just "more is better."** For Conscientiousness, Extraversion, and Emotional Stability, write *very_high* narratives that acknowledge diminishing returns or rigidity as a gentle trade-off, and *very_low* narratives that name compensating strengths (spontaneity, introvert-leadership advantages, etc.).
3. **Separate "success" from "satisfaction" explicitly.** Name the high-Openness "creative but restless" and high-Neuroticism "capable but drained" patterns constructively — as things to actively manage, not fixed fates.
4. **For Agreeableness, lead with the Japan-specific findings, not the Western penalty.** Frame high Agreeableness as a genuine career asset for this audience (income, engagement, and — in collectivist contexts — leadership effectiveness), while still noting the international research is more mixed on promotion specifically.
5. **Replace "find your passion" framing with "develop your passion/skills" growth-mindset framing** throughout.
6. **Avoid gendered claims and dollar-figure specifics.** Use directional language ("tends to be associated with," "on average") rather than named income gaps — the underlying studies are Western, cross-sectional, and not something an individual user's result should be pinned to.
7. **Frame maturation as a running thread.** Whenever a trait level might read as a limitation (low Conscientiousness, high Neuroticism), pair it with the well-evidenced fact that these traits reliably shift with age and experience.

## Caveats

- **Effect sizes are modest.** Even the strongest single-trait validities (Conscientiousness ~.20–.22; leadership Extraversion .31) leave most variance unexplained. Narratives should avoid deterministic language.
- **Directionality/causality is often unclear**, especially for income — success can change personality just as personality can shape success, and family background and ability confound earnings effects.
- **WEIRD-sample bias is pervasive**, and the Japanese/East Asian evidence base — while pointing toward consistent, meaningful reversals — is still comparatively thin. Any Japan-facing narrative should be built with that humility, even while leaning into the reversals as the more locally accurate framing.
- **Single-study findings to hold loosely:** Grant (2013) ambivert advantage; Lee & Ohtake (2012, 2018); Hatano et al. (2026); Kawai et al. (2024); Robie et al. — individual studies, not meta-analyses. Kept for their cultural relevance or hook value, but should be phrased with appropriate hedging in generated copy rather than stated as settled fact.
